<div class="sec dark center"><div class="bar"></div><p class="kicker">Part 2</p><h1>What raters reward</h1><p class="lead">Four components, and none of them is “sound impressive.”</p></div>

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">The four components</p><h2>Every score comes down to four things</h2><div class="cards"><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">1</p><h3>Clear thesis</h3><p class="txt2">Take a position the reader can state back to you after one sentence.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">2</p><h3>Developed support</h3><p class="txt2">Back every claim with reasons and concrete examples, then explain why they prove your point.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">3</p><h3>Organization</h3><p class="txt2">Each paragraph has one job, and each sentence builds on the one before it.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">4</p><h3>Clean language</h3><p class="txt2">Clear, correct sentences. <div class="sec dark center"><div class="bar"></div><p class="kicker">Part 3</p><h1>Your 30 minutes</h1><p class="lead">Plan fast, write most of the time, edit only if you need to.</p></div>

---

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Timing plan</p><h2>Most of your time goes to writing</h2><div class="timeline"><div class="seg s1" v-click><p class="t">3–5 min</p><p class="d">Brainstorm and outline</p></div><div class="seg s2" v-click><p class="t">20–25 min</p><p class="d">Write: intro, two or three body paragraphs, counterpoint, conclusion</p></div><div class="seg s3" v-click><p class="t">0–3</p><p class="d">Edit</p></div></div><p class="explain">You won’t hit the planning time at first. ---

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">A dependable structure</p><h2>Five blocks you can fill on any prompt</h2><div class="outline"><div class="ob" v-click><h3>Intro</h3><p>Frame the issue in a sentence or two, then state your thesis and preview your reasons.</p></div><div class="ob" v-click><h3>Body 1</h3><p>Your strongest reason: a claim, a concrete example, and analysis tying it to the thesis.</p></div><div class="ob" v-click><h3>Body 2</h3><p>A second reason, ideally from a different area: history, science, business, daily life.</p></div><div class="ob" v-click><h3>Counterpoint</h3><p>The best objection, taken seriously, then answered or used to define where your view applies.</p></div><div class="ob" v-click><h3>Conclusion</h3><p>Restate the position, now with the qualifications you’ve earned. No new ideas.</p></div></div><p class="foot">This layout naturally covers “describe when it would or wouldn’t apply” instructions.</p></div>

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">What makes a thesis work</p><h2>A strong thesis is clear, not clever</h2><ul class="points"><li>It takes a position you could argue against</li><li>It follows the instructions: agree or disagree, and to what extent</li><li>It previews your reasons in a few words</li><li>Reusing the prompt’s language is fine. Clarity matters more than originality</li></ul></div>

<div class="sec dark center"><div class="bar"></div><p class="kicker">Part 5</p><h1>Supporting your claims</h1><p class="lead">This is where scores are won: claims, examples and analysis.</p></div>

---
layout: none
---

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Evidence that persuades</p><h2>Lean on reasoning and real-world examples</h2><div class="cards3"><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">1</p><h3>Real-world first</h3><p class="txt2">History, current events, science, business and well-known institutions are concrete and hard to dismiss.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">2</p><h3>Plausible hypotheticals</h3><p class="txt2">A realistic scenario works when it illustrates a mechanism, like how a city budget actually gets spent.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">3</p><h3>Personal, sparingly</h3><p class="txt2">Your own experience can help, but on its own it reads as opinion. Use it to support, not as the whole case.</p></div></div><p class="foot" v-click>Keep the tone academic: no slang, no “like,” no all-caps emphasis.</p></div>

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Weak vs. strong support</p><h2>Same claim, two very different paragraphs</h2><div class="compare"><div class="col"><p class="lbl">Weak: opinion and anecdote</p><p class="passage">I think taking other classes is good for everyone. My cousin was an engineer and she hated her history class but now she says it was useful sometimes. Why would anyone not want to learn more? Many of today’s common jobs, such as data analyst or user-experience designer, combine skills from fields that were once taught separately. An engineering student who has also studied psychology, for example, is better equipped to design products people find easy to use. In this way, breadth is not a distraction from expertise; it is what lets expertise adapt.</p></div></div></div>

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Build every body paragraph the same way</p><h2>Claim → example → analysis → link</h2><ul class="points"><li><b>Claim:</b> one sentence stating this paragraph’s reason</li><li><b>Example:</b> something concrete and specific, not “many people say…”</li><li><b>Analysis:</b> explain <i>why</i> the example proves the claim. This is what separates a 4 from a 3</li><li><b>Link:</b> tie the paragraph back to your thesis</li></ul></div>

<!--

<div class="sec dark center"><div class="bar"></div><p class="kicker">Part 6</p><h1>Organization</h1><p class="lead">Each sentence should earn its place after the one before it.</p></div>

---
layout: none
---

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Two kinds of connection</p><h2>Link ideas within and between paragraphs</h2><div class="cards2"><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">1</p><h3>Within a paragraph</h3><p class="txt2">Each sentence builds on the last. Ask yourself, “How does this sentence move my point forward?” If you can’t say, something is missing.</p></div><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">2</p><h3>Between paragraphs</h3><p class="txt2">Paragraphs progress logically, with a transition that tells the reader where you’re going next.</p></div></div><p class="foot" v-click>Even simple signposts like “First,” “In addition” and “However” show organization. Stronger essays go further and connect ideas, not just list them.</p></div>

---
layout: none
---

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Weak vs. strong organization</p><h2>Watch how each sentence connects to the next</h2><div class="compare"><div class="col"><p class="lbl">Jumps around</p><p class="passage">Colleges should require outside courses. That is why it should be required.</p></div><div class="col good" v-click><p class="lbl">Builds step by step</p><p class="passage">Colleges should require outside courses because employers increasingly value range. A marketing team, for instance, now relies on people who can read data as well as write copy. As a result, a student who has studied both is more valuable from day one. Over time, that advantage compounds, since adaptable employees are the ones trusted with new responsibilities.</p></div></div></div>

<div class="sec "><p class="kicker">Evaluate your own essays</p><h2>Three ways to check where you stand</h2><div class="cards3"><div class="card" v-click><p class="num">1</p><h3>Compare to samples</h3><p class="txt2">ETS publishes scored sample essays with rater commentary.
